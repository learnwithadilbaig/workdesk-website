Late arrival fines: how to write a policy people accept

Fines are the most argued-about line on any payslip. Most of those arguments aren't about the money. They're about whether the rule was clear and applied the same way to everyone.

{{tick}} WorkDesk team·31 October 2026·{{reading}} min read

Ask an HR manager what they dread about payroll day and "fines" comes up a lot. An employee says the machine was down. A manager says he gave verbal permission. Someone else was late more often and wasn't fined. By the end of it, the money involved is tiny and the damage to trust isn't.

The good news is that almost all of this is avoidable with a clear written policy, applied consistently, with the records visible to the person being fined.

First, check what the law allows

In Pakistan, fines and deductions from wages are regulated. The Payment of Wages Act 1936, and the provincial laws that have since replaced or updated it in some provinces, set out when an employer can fine a worker and how much. In broad terms, the rules have expected that:

  • fines are only imposed for acts or omissions listed in a notice that has been approved and displayed at the workplace,
  • the worker gets a chance to explain before a fine is imposed,
  • the total fined in one wage period stays under a legal cap, and
  • fines are recorded in a register.

Deductions for absence from duty are treated differently from fines. Which law applies depends on your province, your industry and the category of worker, so check with your legal adviser or a labour law consultant before you publish a policy.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Labour law in Pakistan is largely provincial and changes over time.

Decide what you're actually trying to fix

A fine is a tool, not a goal. Before writing rules, ask what the lateness costs you. A shop that can't open on time, a production line that can't start, or a customer call nobody answers is a real problem. Someone arriving at 09:07 in a role with no fixed start time probably isn't.

If punctuality matters only for some roles, write the policy for those roles. Applying a factory-floor rule to the whole company creates resentment without improving much.

The parts of a fair policy

A grace period

Traffic exists. A grace period of five to fifteen minutes absorbs ordinary delays and keeps the policy focused on habits rather than accidents.

A threshold before anything happens

Most companies don't fine for the first or second late arrival in a month. A threshold such as "from the third late arrival" separates the occasional bad day from a pattern.

A clear scale

Write down exactly what happens, in numbers. For example, a fixed amount per late arrival after the threshold, or a half-day deduction after a certain number. Vague wording like "appropriate action" is how disputes start.

A cap

Set a maximum per month, and keep it within the legal limit that applies to you. A cap shows the policy is about behaviour, not about recovering salary.

Exceptions and who can grant them

Decide in advance which situations are excused, like approved short leave, official duty outside the office or a recorded machine failure, and who can approve them. Then require the reason to be recorded. An exception with a written reason is fair. An exception without one looks like a favour.

A way to appeal

Give people a short window before payroll closes to dispute a late mark, with a named person who decides. Most disputes disappear when someone can see the actual check-in time.

A sample policy you can adapt

Late arrival policy (example)

Working hours start at 09:00. Check-ins up to 09:10 are within the grace period and are not counted as late.

A check-in after 09:10 counts as a late arrival. The first two late arrivals in a calendar month carry no deduction.

From the third late arrival in a month, a fixed deduction of [amount] applies for each further late arrival, up to a maximum of [amount] per month.

Late arrivals are excused when covered by approved short leave, official duty or a recorded attendance system failure. Excuses must be approved by the line manager in the attendance system with a reason.

Employees can view their late arrivals at any time and may dispute a late mark until the 25th of the month. HR reviews disputes before payroll is run.

Fill in the amounts only after you've checked the legal limits that apply to your workers.

Communicate it before you enforce it

Share the policy in writing, explain it in a team meeting, display it where the law requires, and give people a full month's notice before the first deduction. Then start. A quiet trial month, where people can see what they would have been fined, makes the first real month much calmer.

Apply it the same way to everyone

Consistency is the whole game. That's hard to do by hand: someone has to count late marks from a machine printout, remember who had permission and type deductions into the salary sheet. Each step is a chance for a mistake or an accusation of bias.

Consider the alternatives

Fines aren't the only lever. Some companies get better results from flexible start times for roles that allow it, from a punctuality goal on the performance scorecard, or simply from a manager conversation after a pattern appears. Fines work best as a backstop for roles where being on time really matters.

Summary

  • Check which wage law applies before you write anything.
  • Fine for patterns, not for one bad morning: use a grace period and a threshold.
  • Write the scale and the cap in numbers.
  • Record every exception with a reason.
  • Let employees see their own records and dispute them before payroll.
